# Email Overload: Exploring Personal Information Management of Email
Steve Whittaker, Candace Sidner (1996). CHI '96, ACM. https://www.chi1996.chistatic.hosting.acm.org/proceedings/papers/Whittaker/sw_txt.html
Type: research · Read: 2026-10-06

## What it says
- 34 hours of interviews with 20 office workers at Lotus (9 managers, 9 professionals, 2 assistants), plus mailbox snapshots from 18 of them.

## Findings
- **Email is the work queue.** Users received 49 messages a day on average; one called email the place where "people hand things off to you, it's the electronic office."
- **The inbox is the to-do list.** The mean inbox held 2,482 items (53% of the mailfile) against 858 filed. Users leave to-dos in the inbox as reminders because filing hides them.
- **Threads track status.** The conversation record shows whether one "owes" or "is owed" a response and archives what was discussed.
- **Filing fails.** On average 35% of folders held only one or two items; users postpone filing until they know a message's value.
- **Three strategies, all strained.** No filers (6) kept 3,093.5 inbox items (95% of mail) and relied on search; frequent filers (5) kept 43.4 but paid daily upkeep; spring cleaners (7) kept 1,492.3, and over half their folders failed.
- **Big inboxes lose tasks.** In a large inbox, tasks scroll out of sight and users admit important ones get overlooked.
- **Design asks.** Group threads, mark action items separately from filing, and let users program reminders.

## For internal systems
- Send each assigned task or approval to the user's inbox with a link to the record; the inbox stays where work arrives.
- Give every record a visible "waiting on you" or "waiting on others" status to replace the owe/owed signal of email threads.
- Send reminders for overdue items instead of relying on users to file or recheck.
- Keep comments and decisions on the record so the audit trail no longer depends on someone's inbox.
