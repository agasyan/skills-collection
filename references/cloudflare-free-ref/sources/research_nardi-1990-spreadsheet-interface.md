# The Spreadsheet Interface: A Basis for End User Programming
Bonnie A. Nardi, James R. Miller (1990). Human-Computer Interaction: INTERACT '90, North-Holland. https://www.miramontes.com/writing/spreadsheet-eup/
Type: research · Read: 2026-10-06

## What it says
- Interviews with 11 spreadsheet users in their offices and homes (about 335 transcribed pages), plus walkthroughs of their own spreadsheets.

## Findings
- **Motivation beats cognition.** "The single biggest advantage of spreadsheets is not cognitive but motivational": a few hours of work yields a working model of the user's own problem.
- **Few, high-level functions.** Most users use fewer than ten functions, picked for their domain (SUM over a range instead of a loop).
- **The grid gives structure for free.** Users "do not have to invent a structure"; framing a problem means recognizing where it fits in rows and columns.
- **See everything at once.** Virtually every user valued seeing large amounts of data on one screen without scrolling.
- **Build to think.** Users start entering data before the problem is clear and refine "until I see" the answer. Edits recalculate immediately, and the visible model lets users check accuracy and completeness.
- **A canvas they own.** One user called the spreadsheet a "blank canvas", the opposite of "an artifact created by someone else that must be re-worked before it is of any use."
- **Users are not programmers.** They are domain specialists with no intrinsic interest in computers who need to solve simple domain problems in a few hours.

## For internal systems
- Replacing a sheet takes away a canvas users own; give them a dense, scannable table of their records with sort, filter, and inline edit.
- Show the effect of each edit at once (totals, statuses) so Excel's instant feedback survives.
- Offer a small set of domain actions named in the team's words instead of generic configuration.
- Keep export to Excel so people can still model ad hoc questions the system does not answer.
