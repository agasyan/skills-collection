# Correlations among Prototypical Usability Metrics: Evidence for the Construct of Usability
Jeff Sauro, James R. Lewis (2009). Proceedings of CHI 2009, ACM. https://measuringu.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/01/2009_CorrelationsAmongPrototypicalUsabilityMetrics_EvidenceForTheConstructOfUsability.pdf
Type: research · Read: 2026-10-06

## What it says
- Raw data from 97 datasets (90 usability tests, 1983 to 2008): 2,286 users and 1,034 tasks from 12 sources, including PeopleSoft, Oracle, IBM, Intuit and in-house IT teams. Most tasks were closed-ended work tasks such as expense reports and HR records.
- Median 10 users per test (range 4 to 296). Mean task time 172 s; half of tasks took 90 to 270 s.

## Findings
- **What practitioners measure.** Task time in 99% of datasets, completion 98%, errors 58%, post-test satisfaction 48%, post-task satisfaction 40%.
- **Task metrics move together.** Time and errors r = .60; completion and errors -.54; completion and post-task satisfaction .51; time and post-task satisfaction -.47; completion and time -.46; errors and post-task satisfaction -.44.
- **Ask satisfaction after each task.** End-of-test questionnaires such as SUS tracked task performance weakly (r = .16 to .25).
- **One construct, two factors.** In 325 complete cases all five metrics loaded .63 to .82 on one component (54% of variance); factor analysis split objective measures (completion, time, errors) from subjective ones (satisfaction).
- **Use a standard questionnaire.** Standardized questionnaires were more reliable than homegrown ones (alpha .87 vs .78).
- **Summaries lose detail.** A single composite score keeps about half the variation, so keep the per-metric data.

## For internal systems
- For each task, record completion (yes/no), time in seconds and error count, then ask one satisfaction question straight away.
- Use SUS or another standard questionnaire at the end instead of writing your own.
- Report per-task numbers alongside any single score.
