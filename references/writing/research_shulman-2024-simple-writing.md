# Reading Dies in Complexity: Online News Consumers Prefer Simple Writing
Hillary C. Shulman, David M. Markowitz, Todd Rogers (2024). Science Advances 10(23), eadn2555. https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adn2555
Type: research · Read: 2026-10-06

## What they did
- Analysed over 30,000 headline A/B tests: 7,371 from The Washington Post (2021–22) and 22,664 from Upworthy (2013–15). Preregistered.
- Scored each headline on common words, Flesch readability (words per sentence, syllables per word), analytic style, and length.
- Ran a preregistered experiment with 524 online adults, then repeated it with 249 professional writers and journalists.

## Findings
- **Simpler headlines win clicks.** Within each test, simplicity predicted click-through (Post r = 0.055, Upworthy r = 0.022): small per test, large at scale.
- **Words and readability drive it.** Common words, a less formal style, and higher readability each predicted more clicks; character count did not at the Post.
- **Simple text gets read and remembered.** A simplified headline was picked 34.8% of the time vs 15.3% for the control headline (odds ratio 2.83); recognition was higher (d′ 1.23 vs 0.80, Cohen's d = 0.55).
- **Readers skip complex text.** A complex version was picked less than the control headline (22.2% vs 27.7%).
- **Writers read differently.** Journalists read more carefully overall (d′ 1.43 vs 1.02), showed no simplicity effect, and picked winning headlines at chance (50%).

## For writing docs
- Use common words over formal or specialist synonyms; readers skip complex lines.
- Keep sentences and words short: Flesch readability, built on both, predicted clicks.
- Don't judge clarity by your own reading: expert writers miss what readers skip, so test with real readers.
